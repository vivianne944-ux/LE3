Overview: Diseases of RBC and Bleeding Disorders II

What this lecture covers

  • Anemia due to decreased erythropoiesis: Megaloblastic anemia (Vitamin B12 and Folic acid deficiency), Pernicious anemia, Iron deficiency anemia
  • Anemia of chronic disease: driven by hepcidin
  • Anemias of marrow failure: Aplastic anemia, Myelophthisic anemia, plus anemia of chronic renal failure, diffuse liver disease, neoplasia, hypothyroid disease
  • Evaluation of anemia by RBC indices: MCHC, MCV, MCH patterns
  • Polycythemia: relative vs absolute, Polycythemia vera vs Secondary polycythemia
  • Bleeding disorders: blood vessel, platelet number, platelet function, clotting factors, von Willebrand disease, DIC, clinical syndromes associated with bleeding

Learning objectives

  • To understand the different types of anemia: its causes, symptomatology, laboratory diagnosis based on RBC alterations, and prognosis
  • To understand hemolysis
  • To understand bleeding disorders

Main framework: three questions the lecture keeps asking

1. Why is RBC production low? Nutrient problem (B12, folate, iron), hepcidin block (chronic disease), or marrow failure (aplastic, myelophthisic)
↓
2. What do the RBC indices say? ↓ MCHC / MCV = hemoglobin problem (IDA, thalassemia, ACD). ↑ MCV with high-normal MCHC = B12 / folate / pernicious. Divergent MCHC ↑ with MCV ↓ = spherocytosis
↓
3. If bleeding, which of the three areas is defective? Blood vessel (all parameters normal), Platelet (number ↓ or function defect → ↑ bleeding time), Clotting factors (↑ clotting time, PT or aPTT)

I-A. Megaloblastic Anemia pp. 1–3

Definition and cause

  • Caused by Vitamin B12 and Folic Acid deficiency
    • Lecturer Important substances for DNA synthesis during nuclear maturation

Normal vs Megaloblastic Hematopoiesis

What happens to the cell series

  • The cytoplasm matures normally from blue to pink in both maturations
  • The nucleus remains large and immature throughout the development, in the case of megaloblastic maturation
  • Orthochromatic Normoblast
    • The 4th cell with a pink cytoplasm in both series
    • Normally, it has a pyknotic, dense, small nucleus. In the case of megaloblastic anemia, the nucleus remains large with a coarse chromatin
  • All cells remain large in megaloblastic anemia, including the mature erythrocyte which loses its central pallor when seen on the peripheral smear

Folic acid deficiency

  • Folic acid is derived from vegetables, but it is easily destroyed by heating or cooking
  • Common people at risk
    • Alcoholics: alcohol causes disordered folate metabolism in the liver
    • Pregnancy, Infancy, and Thyrotoxicosis: due to increased requirement
  • Medical conditions that contribute
    • Dialysis and hemolysis
      • There are chronic hemolytic conditions that start as normochromic normocytic but later become macrocytic when the folic acid deficiencies start to appear
    • Cancer patients on methotrexate can also develop folic acid deficiency

Vitamin B12 deficiency

  • Derived from animal products. Vegetarians are at risk
  • Abnormalities of the ileum lead to malabsorption
    • Chronic intestinal lesions such as inflammation or ileitis, lymphoma, and malabsorption syndrome will decrease B12 absorption
  • Surgical removal of either the stomach (which produces IF) or resection of the ileum will also cause B12 deficiency
  • Fish tapeworm (Diphyllobothrium latum) competes with B12 absorption
  • Liver stores large amounts of Vitamin B12. It will take several months before symptoms appear after the complete absence of vitamin B12 in the diet

Laboratory Diagnosis

Lecture-Specific Note Same laboratory abnormalities are present, whether it is B12 or folic acid deficiency.

Peripheral blood smear

  • Macrocytic "hyperchromic" RBC
    • Large red cells compared to small lymphocytes, without central pallor
    • Large nucleated RBC in PBS: "megaloblasts"
      • Normoblasts with immature nuclei may be present
  • Leukopenia with hypersegmented neutrophils or "macropolys" (more than the normal 3–4 lobes)
  • Decreased number of platelets
  • RBC indices
    • Increased MCV
    • High normal MCHC
  • Reticulocyte count: Decreased or Normal
    • In hemolytic anemia, reticulocyte count is always elevated, but in nutritional forms of anemia, the reticulocyte count is either normal or decreased
    • It increases only upon the administration of treatment
      • Striking reticulocytosis: seen at least 5 days after treatment, corresponding to the time needed to stimulate the normoblasts until maturity

Bone marrow

  • Megaloblasts with N:C dissociation
    • Cytoplasm is mature and assumes a pink color but the nucleus is immature showing a large coarse chromatin instead of becoming small, dense, darkly stained
  • Bone marrow hyperplasia
    • Hypercellular: The bone marrow is hypercellular but RBCs are defective and die prior to being released into the peripheral blood, hence the anemia despite the hypercellular marrow
    • Erythroid:Myeloid ratio 1:1
      • Equal volume of erythroid and myeloid precursors in contrast to the normal E:M ratio of 1:4
    • "Megaloblasts"
      • All stages of erythroid development are large with nucleus:cytoplasm asynchronism or dissociation

I-C. Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA) pp. 4–5
Must Know IDA is the most common nutritional disorder.

Causes

Four mechanisms

CauseLecture points
Low dietary intake
  • Best source of iron is heme from animal products.
  • At risk: elderly (avoid meat due to health issues such as hypercholesterolemia, or dentition problems), infants (milk is not a good iron source), marginalized sector (cannot afford meat), teenagers (more junk food than table food).
  • Most common cause of IDA in poor countries
Poor absorption
  • Iron is difficult to absorb and has poor bioavailability, hence it is prescribed with drugs that promote absorption (e.g., ascorbic acid).
  • Patients are advised to avoid substances that inhibit absorption (e.g., tea or carbonated drinks).
  • Heme iron has better bioavailability compared to inorganic iron in vegetables (e.g., malunggay)
Excessive demandPregnant women and children should take iron supplements
Chronic blood loss
  • Most common cause of IDA in developed countries.
  • Occult bleeding results from lesions that cause undetected blood loss for weeks, months, or years wherein iron is not replaced for a long period of time.
  • May occur in malignancies of the bladder, endometrium, or colon, where the blood loss can exit the body unnoticed
Clinical Cue
  • Malignancy is the most common cause of chronic blood loss in the elderly.
  • Hypochromic microcytic anemia in this population should be worked up for possible malignancy.

Iron Metabolism

Two main iron compartments

Functional pool80%
Present in the circulation as hemoglobin, with a small percentage found in myoglobin and a few enzymes
Storage pool20%
Located in the BM or reticuloendothelial system as ferritin or hemosiderin

Absorption, transport, storage, excretion

  • Absorption
    • Serum iron levels are controlled by hepcidin, a micropeptide produced by the liver that exerts several inhibitory mechanisms to prevent iron elevation
      • Inhibits absorption by preventing iron absorbed by the gut epithelium from being released into the circulation
      • Inhibits release of iron from storage or from macrophages to the erythroid precursor
    • Inflammatory mediators (IL-6) increase hepcidin production
      • Responsible for the production of anemia of chronic disease
      • Hepcidin is part of the chemokines that are secreted in inflammation
  • Transport
    • Free serum iron is toxic to the tissues, hence it requires transferrin to carry it in the circulation
  • Storage
    • After 120 days, RBCs are destroyed by the spleen, releasing iron that is then extracted and stored in the liver or BM as ferritin or hemosiderin
  • Excretion
    • Iron is not excreted from the body, although minute amounts may be lost in the gut

Notice: plasma transferrin is the hub between GIT absorption, BM/RES stores and circulation. Hepcidin sits at the GIT → plasma step. Losses are only skin, menses and GI shedding. Fe total body pool: M 3–6 gm, F 2–3 gm.

Peripheral blood smear

  • Morphology: microcytic, hypochromic
    • Reduced production due to abnormalities of hemoglobin
    • Iron deficiency is a problem with heme because this is where iron is located
    • Normal platelets and WBC because they do not contain iron
  • Normal reticulocyte count or normoblasts

Bone marrow

  • Decreased sideroblasts

Iron indices

  • Decreased serum Fe
  • Decreased serum ferritin
    • Decreased stored iron
  • Increased total iron binding capacity (TIBC)

Understanding TIBC

  • TIBC is the measure of the capacity to bind iron. It measures the vacant areas of iron in its transport form
  • In the absence of iron, there would be more vacant spaces and therefore an increased capacity to load or bind more iron
  • Prev Trans The greater the vacant spaces without iron, the greater the tendency of iron binding

Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW)

  • Elevated RDW
    • Indicates an increased amount of poikilocytosis and anisocytosis
  • Prev Trans Can help in differentiating the 2 common causes of hypochromic microcytic anemia (thalassemia and IDA)
  • Reticulocyte (within 3–5 days) increases only upon start of treatment

II. Anemias of Chronic Disease (ACD) p. 5

Pathology

  • Due to increased hepcidin by inflammatory mediators
    • Hepcidin prevents the release of iron for use by progenitor cells despite its abundance in storage
  • Increased hepcidin leads to:
    • Impaired Fe absorption
    • Reduced supply of Fe to erythroid progenitors
    • Impaired Fe utilization

Conditions that develop chronic anemia

  • Chronic immune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis or regional enteritis
  • Neoplasms like malignancy of breast and lungs

Why the TIBC moves the way it does (lecture explanation)

  • IDA: transport protein is deficient in iron
    • Transport protein is deficient of iron making binding to TIBC high
  • Thalassemia: condition with high levels of iron due to hemolysis and frequent transfusion
    • Transport protein is full of iron making TIBC low
  • ACD: hepcidin prevents the absorption and release of iron into the plasma or blood
    • Hepcidin prevents the release of iron from storage and transport protein
    • Iron transfer from one area like plasma storage or protein transport requires energy
      • Decreased in sick people, rendering them unavailable when needed
    • Iron storage (ferritin) and transport are full of iron making TIBC low
Exam Trap: IDA vs ACD
  • Both have low serum iron.
  • The separator is ferritin: high in ACD, low in IDA.
  • TIBC follows: low in ACD, high in IDA.
Study aid (derived from the lecture table) Ferritin tells you where the iron is. Low ferritin = body truly lacks iron (IDA). High ferritin + low serum iron = iron is trapped in storage (ACD). High ferritin + high serum iron = iron overload (thalassemia).

Definition Book

  • Aplastic anemia is a disorder in which multipotent myeloid stem cells are suppressed → bone marrow failure and pancytopenia
  • The marrow is often virtually devoid of recognizable hematopoietic elements

Core Definitions

  • Megaloblastic anemia: caused by Vitamin B12 and Folic acid deficiency, important substances for DNA synthesis during nuclear maturation
  • Pernicious anemia: megaloblastic anemia caused by autoimmune destruction of the gastric mucosa → loss of parietal cells, intrinsic factor, B12 deficiency
  • IDA: most common nutritional disorder
  • ACD: due to increased hepcidin by inflammatory mediators. Hepcidin prevents release of iron for use by progenitor cells despite its abundance in storage
  • Aplastic anemia: multipotent myeloid stem cells are suppressed → bone marrow failure and pancytopenia. Marrow virtually devoid of hematopoietic elements
  • Myelophthisic anemia: extensive infiltration of the marrow by tumors or other lesions (space occupying lesion)
  • Polycythemia: increase in red cells per unit volume of peripheral blood or increase in red cell mass
  • Polycythemia vera: increased red cell mass from autonomous proliferation of myeloid progenitor cells
  • DIC: acute, subacute, chronic thrombo-hemorrhagic disorder occurring as a complication, not a primary disorder

  • dTMP synthesis: needs folate (THF) + B12 (methionine synthase coenzyme). Deficiency of either → ↑ homocysteine → cardiovascular risk (stroke, MI)
  • Propionate pathway: needs B12 only (methylmalonyl-CoA mutase). Deficiency → ↑ methylmalonic acid (neurotoxic) → neurologic abnormalities in B12 deficiency only

Hepcidin

  • Produced by liver, raised by IL-6. Inhibits absorption (gut iron not released to circulation) and inhibits release from storage and macrophages → anemia of chronic disease
  • Free iron is toxic → carried by transferrin. Stored as ferritin or hemosiderin. Iron is not excreted

Aplastic anemia causes

  • Extrinsic (more common): drugs, chemicals, infection → immune mediated (Th1 cytokines kill progenitors). Chloramphenicol notorious. Carbamazepine, phenytoin. Benzene, paints, thinners, insecticides (painters, farmers). Parvovirus highest risk
  • Intrinsic: Fanconi's anemia, defect in DNA repair gene → innate stem cell defect. Telomerase defect 5–10%

Classic Associations

  • Fish tapeworm (Diphyllobothrium latum) → B12 deficiency
  • Methotrexate → folic acid antagonist. Dialysis → folate loss. Alcoholics → disordered folate metabolism
  • Chronic hemolysis → starts normochromic normocytic, later macrocytic when folate deficiency appears
  • Occult bleeding (bladder, endometrium, colon malignancy) → most common cause of IDA in developed countries. Low intake → poor countries
  • Ascorbic acid promotes iron absorption. Tea, carbonated drinks inhibit. Heme iron > inorganic (malunggay)
  • Chloramphenicol → aplastic anemia (notorious). Parvovirus → highest risk
  • Chronic renal failure → ↓ EPO. Liver disease → ↓ coagulation factors, folate deficiency, ↓ phospholipid. Neoplasia → tumor uses nutrients. Hypothyroid → ↓ metabolic activity
  • Spherocytosis → divergence: MCHC ↑, MCV ↓, absent central pallor
  • Renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma → EPO-secreting (secondary polycythemia)
  • Gaisböck syndrome → stress polycythemia
  • Waterhouse Friderichsen → N. meningitidis → adrenal hemorrhage → shock or DIC. Kasabach Merritt → giant hemangiomas → stasis → DIC
  • Obstetric complications, neoplasms, infections (endotoxin via F VIII), massive tissue injury → DIC

Warnings and Treatment Points

Warnings
  • ACD: treat underlying condition first before iron therapy.
  • ITP: platelet transfusion is contraindicated (autoantibodies destroy transfused platelets).
  • Dengue: platelet transfusion not carried out until below 20,000.
  • Aplastic anemia: transplant / immunosuppressive therapy. Does not cause splenomegaly.
  • TTP: plasma exchange. Acute ITP (severe): steroids.
  • PCV: regular phlebotomy extends life. Untreated, death from bleeding or thrombosis.
  • DIC: terminal event after thrombosis is bleeding resulting to death in most patients.

If You See X → Think Y

  • Large nucleus + pink cytoplasm in erythroid precursor → N:C dissociation, megaloblastic anemia
  • Hypersegmented neutrophil + macrocytes without central pallor → megaloblastic anemia
  • Numbness, sensory ataxia, spastic paraparesis + macrocytosis → B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration)
  • Beefy tongue + achlorhydria + serum antibodies → pernicious anemia
  • Koilonychia, pica, esophageal webs → IDA (Plummer Vinson)
  • Elderly with hypochromic microcytic anemia → work up for malignancy (occult blood loss)
  • Low Fe + high ferritin + low TIBC + chronic infection or RA → ACD
  • Pancytopenia + ↓ reticulocytes despite anemia + fatty marrow → aplastic anemia
  • Farmer or painter with pancytopenia → aplastic anemia (insecticides, benzene)
  • Tear drop RBC + leukoerythroblastosis + known cancer → myelophthisic anemia
  • MCHC ↑ but MCV ↓, no central pallor → spherocytosis
  • Plethoric male, pruritus after bath, Hct > 60%, low EPO → polycythemia vera
  • High Hct + high EPO + highlands or renal cell carcinoma → secondary polycythemia
  • Massive splenomegaly late in PCV → spent phase, myelofibrosis, extramedullary hematopoiesis
  • Petechiae with all bleeding tests normal → vessel wall (non-thrombocytopenic purpura)
  • Child, 2 weeks after viral infection, low platelets → acute ITP
  • Low platelets + ↑ megakaryocytes in marrow → ITP (peripheral destruction)
  • Female in 40s, fever, neuro deficits, renal failure, fragmented RBC → TTP (ADAMTS13)
  • Same picture, worse kidneys, no neuro, E. coli → HUS
  • Normal platelet count + ↑ BT + ↑ aPTT + reduced ristocetin → vWD
  • Hemarthrosis, normal PT, ↑ aPTT, normal platelets and BT → Hemophilia A (F VIII)
  • Bleeding after dental extraction, no petechiae → clotting factor defect
  • Sepsis or obstetric complication + all coagulation parameters ↑ + ↓ platelets + fragmented RBC → acute DIC
  • Meningococcemia + adrenal hemorrhage → Waterhouse Friderichsen
  • Giant hemangiomas + bleeding → Kasabach Merritt
